## Should you bother in year one?

Honestly, for most crops, no. Radishes, lettuce, spinach, beans, peas, carrots, zucchini and cucumbers are all better direct sown — the fast ones gain nothing from a head start, and root crops resent transplanting outright.

Starting seeds indoors is worth it for four things:

- **Crops that need a long head start:** tomatoes, peppers, onions.
- **Cost.** A $3 packet replaces $100 of nursery seedlings.
- **Variety.** A garden centre stocks maybe six tomatoes; seed catalogues stock hundreds.
- **Season length.** In zones 3 to 5, a 6-week head start is the difference between ripe tomatoes and green ones at first frost.

If this is your first spring, buy tomato and pepper seedlings and direct sow everything else. Come back to this page in autumn and plan properly for next year.

## The timeline

Every date is counted backwards from your average last spring frost. Get that date from the [planting calendar for your zone](/planting-calendar/), then subtract.

The most common error is starting too early. An eight-week-old tomato in a 3-inch pot with nowhere to go becomes root-bound and stressed, and it will be outperformed by one sown three weeks later. If a cold spring pushes your planting date back, that head start turns into a liability.

## Equipment that matters

You can spend a fortune here. You need very little.

**A light. This is the non-negotiable one.** An ordinary LED shop light, $25 to $40, hung so it sits **2 to 4 inches above the leaves** and run **14 to 16 hours a day** on a cheap timer. You do not need a purple "full spectrum" grow light; a plain 4000K to 6500K LED works. The light must be raised as plants grow to hold that gap.

A windowsill will not do it. Early-spring window light is weak, short and comes from one side, and seedlings respond by stretching toward it into pale, floppy stems that never fully recover.

**Seed-starting mix,** not potting mix and not garden soil. It is finer and drains faster, which directly reduces damping off — the fungal collapse at the stem base that is the leading cause of seedling death.

**Containers with drainage.** Cell trays, 3-inch pots, or yoghurt pots with holes punched in the bottom. All fine. A tray underneath to water into.

**A heat mat,** $15 to $25, for peppers specifically. Both peppers and tomatoes germinate reliably at **75 to 85&deg;F** soil temperature, warmer than most rooms. It roughly halves pepper germination time. Take it away once seedlings emerge.

**A small fan** on low for a few hours a day. Air movement thickens stems and dries the surface between waterings, which discourages fungus. Optional but cheap and effective.

Skip: heated propagators with domed lids left on too long, expensive branded grow lights, peat pellets, and anything sold as a "kit" at four times the component cost.

## Sowing, step by step

1. **Moisten the mix before filling.** Damp like a wrung-out sponge, not wet. Dry mix repels water and floats seed around when you try to fix it later.
2. **Fill and firm gently.** Leave a quarter inch below the rim.
3. **Sow two seeds per cell** at the depth in the table above — about a quarter inch for tomatoes, peppers and onions. Two per cell hedges against poor germination; snip the weaker one at soil level later rather than pulling it and disturbing roots.
4. **Label everything now.** Every seedling looks identical at two weeks and you will not remember.
5. **Cover to hold humidity** until germination — a lid or loose plastic. Remove it the moment sprouts appear, or you invite damping off.
6. **Light on as soon as the first seedling breaks the surface**, 2 to 4 inches above the leaves.
7. **Water from below** where you can: pour into the tray and let the mix wick up. It keeps stems dry and encourages roots downward.

Germination times vary widely — tomatoes in 5 to 10 days, peppers 10 to 21, kale 5 to 8, onions 7 to 14. Peppers are slow enough that people assume failure at two weeks. Wait three.

## Aftercare

**Thin to one per cell** once seedlings have their first true leaves — the second pair, which look like the adult plant, as distinct from the rounded seed leaves.

**Pot up when roots fill the cell**, usually 3 to 4 weeks in. Tomatoes especially benefit from moving into a 4-inch pot, and you can bury the stem deeper each time to fix legginess.

**Start feeding at two to three weeks** with a half-strength liquid fertilizer once a week. Seed-starting mix holds almost no nutrients; the seed carries the plant for the first fortnight and then it needs feeding.

**Let the surface dry slightly between waterings.** Constantly saturated mix is the single biggest damping-off risk. If seedlings collapse at the base overnight, that is what happened — improve airflow, water less, and do not re-use unsterilised trays.

## Hardening off

This is where most indoor-raised seedlings die, and it is entirely avoidable. Indoor leaves have no wind conditioning and no UV tolerance. Put them straight into full sun and they scorch or wilt past recovery within a day or two.

Give it **7 to 10 days**:

- **Days 1 to 2:** 1 to 2 hours outside, in full shade, out of wind. Bring them in.
- **Days 3 to 4:** 3 to 4 hours, with an hour of morning sun.
- **Days 5 to 6:** most of the day, half of it in sun.
- **Days 7 to 8:** full day in full sun. Water more — outdoor air dries pots fast.
- **Days 9 to 10:** leave them out overnight, provided the night stays above 50&deg;F.

Then transplant, ideally on an overcast day or in late afternoon, and water in immediately. Hold off if a cold snap is forecast: tomatoes want nights above 50&deg;F and peppers sulk below 55&deg;F.

A cold frame or an unheated porch shortens the whole process and saves the daily carrying, but a couple of trays moved on and off a step works perfectly well.
