## The 12 crops ranked

Difficulty here means how many ways there are to get it wrong: whether it needs starting indoors, whether it needs support, how fussy germination is, and how much it minds inconsistent watering.

## 1. Radish — 22 to 30 days

The fastest thing you can grow and the best possible first sowing. Seeds go in half an inch deep, an inch or two apart, and germinate in **3 to 7 days**. They handle frost, so you can sow 4 to 2 weeks before your last frost date.

Sow a short row every two weeks rather than all at once — a whole row maturing at the same moment is more radishes than anyone wants. Pull them as soon as the shoulders swell; left too long they turn pithy. Avoid sowing in midsummer, when heat makes them woody and split.

## 2. Spinach — 40 to 50 days

Spinach handles cold better than almost anything else in the garden and germinates in soil as cool as **40&deg;F**, which makes it one of the first crops you can sow in spring. Sow half an inch deep, thin to 3 to 4 inches.

Its one weakness is heat: once days lengthen and temperatures climb, spinach bolts and turns bitter fast. Treat it as a spring and autumn crop and sow again 10 to 12 weeks before your first fall frost.

## 3. Leaf lettuce — 45 to 55 days

Choose leaf lettuce over head varieties. Leaf types are cut-and-come-again: take the outer leaves, leave the growing centre, and one plant feeds you for weeks.

The trick most packets underplay is that **lettuce seed needs light to germinate**. Press it into damp soil and barely dust it over — bury it a half inch and it will not come up. Thin to 8 to 12 inches, and keep it in the shade of a taller crop once summer arrives to delay bolting.

## 4. Zucchini — 45 to 55 days

Almost aggressively productive. Two plants feed a household, and this is not a figure of speech — plant six and you will be leaving bags on neighbours' porches.

Direct sow an inch deep, 2 to 3 weeks after your last frost, once soil is above **70&deg;F**. Give each plant 24 to 36 inches. Pick fruit at 6 to 8 inches; leave one on the plant and it becomes a baseball bat while signalling the plant to stop producing. Check for squash bugs on stems weekly.

## 5. Bush beans — 50 to 60 days

Bush beans need no trellis, no transplanting and no thinning fuss. Sow an inch deep, 3 to 4 inches apart, in rows 18 inches apart, and they crop heavily from a small space.

The one rule: **wait for warm soil**. Below 60&deg;F the seed sits and rots. That usually means 1 to 2 weeks after last frost, not the week of it. Pick every two or three days once they start — regular picking is what keeps new pods setting.

## 6. Kale — 50 to 65 days

Kale is the longest-serving plant in a beginner bed. Harvest leaf by leaf from the bottom up and a single plant produces for months, straight through light frosts that improve the flavour.

Direct sow a quarter to half an inch deep, or start indoors 6 to 4 weeks before last frost. Thin to 12 to 18 inches. The only real pest problem is cabbage worms, which lace the leaves — check undersides weekly and pick them off by hand.

## 7. Peas — 60 to 70 days

Peas are among the first things you can plant, going in **6 to 4 weeks before your last frost** once soil reaches 45&deg;F. Sow an inch deep, 2 to 3 inches apart.

The mistake to avoid is trellising late. Peas climb from the start, and threading a tangled mat onto netting later is miserable — put the support in the day you sow. They fade once summer heat arrives; pull them and plant beans in the space.

## 8. Cucumber — 55 to 65 days

Cucumbers are easy in the ground and much easier on a trellis, where fruit hangs straight, stays clean and takes almost no footprint. Sow an inch deep 2 to 3 weeks after last frost, in soil above 70&deg;F.

They dislike root disturbance, so direct sow if you can rather than transplanting. Bitter fruit is nearly always a watering problem — cucumbers are mostly water and need it consistently, roughly an inch a week without long dry gaps.

## 9. Tomato — 60 to 85 days from transplant

The crop most people start a garden for, and the one where homegrown beats the supermarket by the widest margin. In year one, buy a seedling for $4 to $6 rather than starting seed 8 weeks early.

Two things matter. **Plant deep** — bury the bottom third of the stem, stripping the lowest leaves, and it will root along the buried section. And **install the cage at planting**, not in July. Choose a cherry variety for your first attempt: they ripen earlier, crack less and crop for months. Note that the 60 to 85 days is counted from transplanting, not from seed.

## 10. Carrot — 65 to 80 days

Carrots earn their place on flavour, not ease. Nothing from a shop tastes like one pulled the same day.

They are ranked tenth because germination is genuinely fussy: seed goes a quarter inch deep and **the surface must stay damp for up to two weeks**. Let it crust dry and nothing comes up. Cover the row with burlap or a board until sprouts appear. Soil matters too — rocky or heavy ground gives forked, stubby roots, so this is the crop that most rewards a raised bed. Thin to 2 to 3 inches or you get green tops and no root.

## The two to leave until next year

**Peppers** (70 to 90 days) and **onions** (100 to 120 days) are not difficult to grow, but both need 8 to 10 weeks indoors under a light before they can go out. That makes them a seed-starting project rather than a first-sowing project. Onions add a second trap: you have to buy the right day-length type for your latitude — long-day north of roughly 36&deg;N, short-day south of it — or you get greens and no bulb.

Both become straightforward once you have read [starting seeds indoors](/starting-seeds-indoors/). You can also buy pepper seedlings in year one and grow them exactly like tomatoes, just planted 2 to 3 weeks later, since they sulk below 55&deg;F at night.
